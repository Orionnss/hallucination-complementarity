\paragraph{Voter Quality and Ensemble Gains:}
Characterising each subset by the published quality of its members and by their mutual
redundancy separates two questions that are easily conflated, and
Table~\ref{tab:quality_gain} reports both. A subset's absolute score is largely fixed by its
strongest constituent: the rank correlation between the best member's published figure and
the combination's is $\rho = +0.80$ on MCC and $+0.81$ on AUROC, computed within each
generator and averaged, against only $\rho = +0.59$ for the number of voters. To anticipate
how a combination will perform it therefore suffices to know which detector in it is best.
The \emph{increment} over that member is a different matter, and most candidate explanations
fail not because their coefficients are small but because their sign is unstable: quality
levels and pairwise $\kappa$ each agree in sign on only three or four of the six generators,
so their averages should not be read as effects. The one predictor negative in all six is
the \emph{spread} of member quality, the standard deviation of the members' published
scores, at $\rho = -0.50$ on MCC and $-0.56$ on AUROC: subsets whose members are evenly
matched gain more from combining than subsets pairing one strong detector with weaker ones.
That relationship is not a proxy for containing a weak member, since the weakest member's
quality barely predicts gain on its own ($\rho = +0.04$) and the partial correlation
strengthens to $-0.47$ once it is controlled for. Table~\ref{tab:spread_example} shows the
effect directly --- holding SAPLMA fixed and varying its partner, gain falls from $+0.055$ to
$+0.039$ as the spread widens --- and it is the one place in this study where the standard
variance-reduction argument for ensembling partially holds, at a magnitude that leaves the
best member's own score doing almost all the work.
