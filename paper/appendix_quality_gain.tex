\section{Voter quality and ensemble gain}
\label{sec:appendix:quality_gain}

Each subset of size $k \ge 2$ is characterised by the published quality of its members
and by their mutual redundancy, and these are correlated against two outcomes: the
subset's absolute soft-vote score, and its \emph{gain} over the strongest member it
contains. Quality is the member's own published figure --- MCC from its stored decision,
AUROC from its continuous score --- so the predictor is always in the same units as the
outcome it is compared with. Redundancy is the mean pairwise Cohen's $\kappa$ among the
subset's members. There are 156 subsets per metric ($26$ per generator $\times$ $6$
generators); each cell reports Spearman $\rho$ followed by Pearson $r$.

The two outcomes behave differently, and separating them is the point of this appendix: a
subset's absolute score is largely fixed by its best member, whereas what little variation
there is in the \emph{increment} over that member is governed by something else entirely.

An ensemble's score is therefore largely determined by its strongest constituent,
at $\rho \approx 0.80$ within a generator rather than the $\rho \approx 0.96$ the
pooled figure suggests. The association with mean pairwise $\kappa$ is a corollary rather
than an independent effect: redundancy and strength travel together in this pool, so
redundant subsets score well without redundancy contributing anything.

\subsection{What spread measures}

Spread is the standard deviation of the members' published MCC within a single
subset, so it measures how evenly matched the voters are rather than how good they are. A
low spread indicates members of comparable strength; a high spread indicates one strong
detector accompanied by weaker ones.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\footnotesize
  \caption{Worked example on Qwen3-14B, whose five detectors score SAPLMA $0.483$,
  LapEigvals $0.468$, Attn.\ $0.427$, ICR $0.398$ and SVD $0.332$.}
  \begin{tabular}{l l c c c}
    \hline
    Subset & Member MCCs & Spread & Ensemble & Gain \\
    \hline
    SAPLMA+LapEig. & 0.483, 0.468 & 0.0072 & 0.5378 & $+0.0551$ \\
    LapEig.+Attn. & 0.468, 0.426 & 0.0209 & 0.4857 & $+0.0175$ \\
    SAPLMA+Attn. & 0.483, 0.426 & 0.0281 & 0.5271 & $+0.0444$ \\
    SAPLMA+ICR & 0.483, 0.398 & 0.0424 & 0.5230 & $+0.0403$ \\
    SAPLMA+SVD & 0.483, 0.332 & 0.0752 & 0.5217 & $+0.0391$ \\
    SAPLMA+LapEig.+Attn. & 0.483, 0.468, 0.426 & 0.0238 & 0.5353 & $+0.0526$ \\
    SAPLMA+ICR+SVD & 0.483, 0.398, 0.332 & 0.0616 & 0.5224 & $+0.0397$ \\
    \hline\end{tabular}\end{table}

Spread is correlated with the weakest member's quality at $\rho =
-0.605$, which raises the objection that it is merely a proxy
for containing a weak detector. That objection does not hold. The weakest member's quality
on its own correlates with gain at only $\rho = +0.075$, and when
gain and spread are both residualised on it the partial correlation \emph{strengthens},
from $\rho = -0.430$ to $\rho =
-0.466$. What matters is the mismatch
between voters rather than the presence of a weak one, which is also why the correlation is
around $-0.43$ rather than near $-0.9$: LapEigvals$+$Attn.\ combines low spread with low
gain and is a genuine counterexample.

The mechanism is the ordinary ensembling argument. Soft voting averages with equal weight,
so a substantially weaker member pulls the mean toward its own errors and the combination
spends its capacity recovering ground the strongest member already held, whereas matched
members allow independent errors to cancel. This is one of the few places in this study
where that argument partially holds, and the effect remains small in absolute terms.
